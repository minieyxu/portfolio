finding a hotel

How do interface choices shape the path from browsing to booking?

Role
Interface analysis & concept sketches
Method
Cognitive-principle evaluation
Context
SI 347 · Individual coursework
Scope
Proposed redesigns · Not yet user-tested

what makes the next step clear?

I compared Super 8 and Ritz-Carlton to examine what visitors need to notice, interpret, and remember while browsing hotels. I looked at navigation, search controls, promotions, destination lists, and ways to return to a hotel.

I used five cognitive principles to explain potential usability concerns, then sketched targeted modifications. For each proposal, I considered its intended benefit and what it might make harder to discover.

Design question

How might the interface help someone find a hotel while making the next step easier to understand?

A clearer primary task should still leave useful options discoverable.

This is an individual academic evaluation. The concepts have not yet been tested with users.

how I evaluated the interfaces

I compared the pages through five cognitive principles, identified potential concerns, and proposed a modification with a clear trade-off. The task framing connects each change to what someone might be trying to do.

Two ways into hotel browsing

  • Super 8

    I focused on starting a search: booking controls, secondary travel categories, and the promotional call to action.

  • Ritz-Carlton

    I focused on exploring geographic destination lists and a proposed way to save hotels for a later visit.

Read the five cognitive principles
Signifiers
Signifiers communicate where and how an interaction can happen. Here, the cue needs to communicate more than clickability: it should also help set an expectation about what the click will do.1
Hick’s Law
Hick’s Law relates the number of alternatives to decision time. I used it as a reason to examine how many choices appear at once. It does not establish that removing any navigation item will make a real task faster; familiarity, grouping, and the visitor’s goal still matter.2
Selective attention & salience
Salience describes how strongly an element stands out. Color, size, contrast, and placement can attract attention even when an element is secondary to the visitor’s immediate goal. I used this principle to examine which action was most visually prominent.3
Visual structure
Visual structure uses grouping, alignment, spacing, and hierarchy to communicate relationships. Making the region visually distinct could help a visitor narrow attention to one relevant group before reading its destinations.4
Long-term memory
Long-term memory is imperfect. A persistent saved list would act as an external memory aid, allowing someone to recognize a property they liked rather than recall its name and location unaided.4

what I would change

Five proposals connect an interface observation to a cognitive principle, a design decision, and a trade-off.

  1. Proposal 1 · Signifiers

    Know where a link will lead.

    The travel-category bar placed internal options alongside links that led to another website. In my analysis, the similar presentation did not clearly signal that difference before a visitor clicked.

    I proposed adding an external-link icon to off-site options such as Cruises, while keeping internal options unchanged. The intent was to help visitors anticipate a change in destination.

    Select to enlarge

    Why this change—and what it could cost

    The cognitive principle

    Signifiers communicate where and how an interaction can happen. Here, the cue needs to communicate more than clickability: it should also help set an expectation about what the click will do.1

    Design rationale

    An external-link cue makes a difference in behavior visible at the point of choice. It could support more predictable navigation without adding a separate explanation for every category.

    The trade-off

    Icons add visual complexity, and their meaning may be unfamiliar. I would check whether visitors understand the cue and whether a short text label would communicate it more clearly.

    What I would test: Before clicking, can someone correctly predict which options stay on the hotel site and which lead elsewhere?

    This and the next proposal are alternatives for the same category bar: add cues if the bar remains visible, or move the categories into Travel Bundles.

  2. Proposal 2 · Hick’s Law

    Give hotel search a clearer starting point.

    Someone arriving to book a hotel encounters destination, date, guest, and rate controls alongside a second row of travel categories. Those categories offer useful services, but they also introduce other directions at the start of the hotel-search task.

    I proposed removing that category row from the booking area and keeping those services under the existing Travel Bundles navigation. This preserves access while changing when the options are encountered.

    Select to enlarge

    Why this change—and what it could cost

    The cognitive principle

    Hick’s Law relates the number of alternatives to decision time. I used it as a reason to examine how many choices appear at once. It does not establish that removing any navigation item will make a real task faster; familiarity, grouping, and the visitor’s goal still matter.2

    Design rationale

    The proposed entry point emphasizes the information needed for a hotel search. Visitors seeking another travel service would still have a route through the main navigation.

    The trade-off

    Flights, cruises, and bundles could become less discoverable. A focused booking area is only useful if visitors can still find the services they came for.

    What I would test: Compare starting a hotel search and finding a flight-and-hotel bundle. Observe hesitation, wrong turns, task completion, and whether the secondary service remains discoverable.

  3. Proposal 3 · Selective attention & salience

    Let the primary action hold the spotlight.

    The hotel-search controls appeared near a large offer panel with its own Learn More button. I saw a potential competition between the visitor’s booking task and the page’s promotional message.

    I changed the promotional button into a smaller text link and positioned it below the search action. The proposal changes the relative emphasis of the promotion while leaving a way to explore it.

    Select to enlarge

    The promotional view is an original coursework sketch exploring hierarchy rather than a finished visual design.

    Why this change—and what it could cost

    The cognitive principle

    Salience describes how strongly an element stands out. Color, size, contrast, and placement can attract attention even when an element is secondary to the visitor’s immediate goal. I used this principle to examine which action was most visually prominent.3

    Design rationale

    A quieter promotion could make the search controls easier to prioritize. The goal is a clearer relationship between the primary task and an optional offer.

    The trade-off

    Visitors may miss a relevant discount, and the offer may receive less engagement. I would evaluate the ability to find an offer as well as the ability to start a booking.

    What I would test: Ask visitors to begin a search and then locate the offer. Observe which action they notice first and whether the promotion remains findable.

  4. Proposal 4 · Visual structure

    See the region. Then scan the destinations.

    Ritz-Carlton’s destination list used differences in font size and weight to distinguish geographic headings from individual locations. In the long list I reviewed, those differences felt subtle when scanning across multiple columns.

    I added a light background highlight behind geographic headings while leaving the location names unchanged. The proposal strengthens the distinction between a category and the entries within it.

    Select to enlarge

    Why this change—and what it could cost

    The cognitive principle

    Visual structure uses grouping, alignment, spacing, and hierarchy to communicate relationships. Making the region visually distinct could help a visitor narrow attention to one relevant group before reading its destinations.4

    Design rationale

    A visitor looking for California could identify the heading first and then scan the places beneath it. The change works within the existing geographic organization rather than introducing a different navigation system.

    The trade-off

    Repeated highlights could add clutter or compete with other elements. I would compare this approach with stronger spacing or typography to see which makes the hierarchy clearest.

    What I would test: Ask visitors to locate a destination in a named region. Compare search time and wrong selections across the original and proposed hierarchy.

  5. Proposal 5 · Long-term memory

    Make it easier to pick up where you left off.

    Hotel browsing may take place across more than one visit. In the pages I reviewed, I did not find an option for saving individual properties, so returning to a hotel of interest could require remembering its name or repeating the search.

    I proposed heart controls for individual hotels and a Saved item in the main navigation. The original assignment sketch illustrates the navigation entry; the small walkthrough below makes the proposed save-and-return interaction more concrete.

    Select to enlarge

    Why this change—and what it could cost

    The cognitive principle

    Long-term memory is imperfect. A persistent saved list would act as an external memory aid, allowing someone to recognize a property they liked rather than recall its name and location unaided.4

    Design rationale

    The same hotel remains recognizable when someone returns to the list. The proposed feature shifts part of the remembering task from the visitor to the interface.

    The trade-off

    Heart controls and a new navigation item add complexity. The concept also needs decisions about guest access, persistence, removal, and how clearly the saved state is communicated.

    What I would test: Can someone save a hotel, find it again, understand whether it is still saved, and remove it without confusion?

what I learned & what I’d do next

The recurring question across these proposals is how much the interface should show at once. Making hotel search more prominent could help someone focus, while making a bundle or discount less noticeable. Adding saved hotels could support memory, while introducing another feature to understand.

Working through each principle made the reasoning behind a small modification more explicit. Next, I would compare the original and proposed interfaces using realistic tasks, checking whether the primary task becomes clearer and whether useful secondary options remain discoverable.

Questions for the next evaluation

Can visitors anticipate an external link, start a hotel search, find a destination, and return to a saved hotel? I would observe hesitation, wrong turns, task completion, and expectations about the interface.

Scope, limitations & references

The concerns come from inspecting captured interface states, rather than participant sessions. The modifications are independent sketches, including alternative approaches to the same navigation bar. They do not represent an implemented redesign or measured improvement.
